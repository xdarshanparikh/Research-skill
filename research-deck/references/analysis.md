# Analysis: from facts to a storyline

## Data integrity
- **Recreate charts from the numbers.** Don't paste third-party chart images. If a published figure must be used as-is, cite it exactly and prefer openly licensed ones.
- **Cross-check totals.** Add up the parts from the primary source before printing a total. News reports often round, blend or misquote: one news site said "~53,000" where the budget documents summed to 72,697.
- **Percentages are computed by you** from primary values and add up to 100 (`charts.py` uses largest-remainder rounding). Say when an allocation mixes money types (e.g. "20,000 from bonds + 786 from the budget").
- **When two official totals differ** (e.g. sector data 69.0 vs supply 68.8), scale one to match or say so in the notes.
- **Label derived numbers.** Record the formula in the speaker notes. Mention it in the footer when it changes the meaning ("2025 supplier shares applied to FY26").
- **Date every number.** Refresh anything older than about 12 months. If a series has truly stopped (e.g. it ends in 2021), keep the year visible, write "(latest available)" and tell the user.
- **Compare like with like:** the same period, unit, currency year (nominal vs real) and geography. Say which one you used.

## The Fact Sheet
The user steers the content before any slide exists.
- **Story draft:** 1–2 sentences on what the evidence says. The user can change it.
- **Themes:** what the audience needs to understand, in the order they'd need it. These are a draft of the storyline drawn from the research, not from a template.
- **Facts:** 20–35 in all. Tick the strongest 2–4 per theme so every theme is covered, and leave the rest visible but unticked. Each fact has its number, plain meaning, source plus link, year, "newer edition checked?" (start with ✗ when it's old and nothing newer exists) and the chart data it carries.
- **Leads:** angles found but not researched (a case study, a new policy, a competitor). The user ticks the ones to dig into.
- After the picks come back: research the ticked leads and added topics, fix what they commented on, rebuild the sheet under a new name if much changed, and only then move on.

## Storyline
- Find the story after research: list what the audience needs to know, in order, and give each slide the one visual that shows its point best. Don't force a fixed shape on every topic.
- Offer **2–3 genuinely different storylines**, for example:
  - **Situation → complication → resolution** (a classic briefing)
  - **Answer first**, then the supporting evidence (for executives)
  - **Journey over time:** then → now → next
  - **Comparison:** us vs peers, option A vs B vs C
- For each slide, give the **takeaway title**, the **chart type** and the **fact IDs**, so every visual traces back to a picked fact.
- Include an **implications or "so what"** slide where it fits the topic: who gains, who loses, what to do, with named real examples. Its form and place follow the story; it isn't a fixed last slide.
- Offer 3 closing messages in different tones (a reflective takeaway, a clear recommendation, an open question).

## Words
- Plain words with the number in them ("3.8x more demand than supply", not "heavily oversubscribed").
- No invented metaphors and no filler qualifiers ("(latest data)", "a staggering").
- Spell out each abbreviation the first time it's used. Write source names the way the publisher writes them.
